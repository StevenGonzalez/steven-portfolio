Virtual Trainer has an AI trainer. You can ask it about your progress or your plan, and it answers from your own workout history instead of giving generic fitness advice. Building it shaped how I think about AI in products in general.

## Start from the job

I start with what the person is trying to do, not with where a model could fit.

Explaining, summarizing, drafting and answering questions about your own data are things language models are good at. Exact transactions, strict business rules and anything that has to come out the same every time are not.

In Virtual Trainer that line is clear. Workout history, timers, completed sets, account state and progression are plain code. The AI trainer reads from them and explains them. It doesn't own them.

## Context is the feature

A model with no context gives the answer it would give anyone. That's easy to build and doesn't help much.

What makes the trainer useful is that it answers from the user's own training data. Choosing what goes into that context is engineering work, more than prompt wording: what's relevant to this question, what's out of date, and what's sensitive enough that it shouldn't be sent at all. More data doesn't automatically mean a better answer.

## Keep the rules in code

The model can interpret, suggest, summarize and explain. The app validates, saves, authorizes, calculates and enforces rules. If a model suggests a change, the change goes through the same checks as anything else before it touches real data.

This matters most where the product already has rules. Automatic progression in Virtual Trainer has limits, so it can adapt without suggesting unsafe jumps. A model shouldn't invent a second progression system next to that one. It can explain what the rules decided and why, and help someone choose between options that are already safe.

## Easy to trust, easy to ignore

People trust an AI feature when they can see what it's doing. They should know when they're reading generated text and what it's based on. "Based on your last four workouts" is easier to believe than advice that comes from nowhere. When the system isn't sure, it should say so. When it can't answer safely, it should say that instead of reaching.

There should always be a way out too: look at the underlying data, edit what was generated, or skip the suggestion entirely. A generated plan that someone reviews before it's applied is safer than one that quietly changes their account.

## Test the behavior, not the demo

Looking good in a demo doesn't tell you much. Collect real examples: normal questions, edge cases, things it should refuse, and answers that came out wrong. Run them again whenever the prompt, the context or the model changes. That doesn't need a framework on day one, just the habit.

Check the outcome as well as the text. Did the answer help? Did it stay inside the product's rules? When it failed, could the UI show that clearly? I hold the rest of the app to the same questions.
